THE #1 STYLE RULE — MODERN 2D CARTOON EXPLAINER
=====================================================================

The entire image must look like a polished, colorful 2D cartoon illustration made for a modern educational, documentary, business, historical, or storytelling video.

The target visual language is a simplified, expressive cartoon — NOT a doodle, NOT a whiteboard sketch, and NOT a realistic illustration.

Think:

- clean hand-illustrated 2D cartoon artwork
- bold dark black or near-black outlines
- smooth, controlled linework with slight organic variation
- colorful, balanced fills
- expressive cartoon characters
- simplified but believable cartoon anatomy
- readable props and environments
- clear foreground, middle-ground and background separation
- subtle 2D cartoon shading and highlights
- warm, friendly, story-driven visual design
- polished enough to feel like a finished animated explainer frame

Characters should feel like modern cartoon people: simplified, expressive and visually memorable, but NOT chibi, childlike, overly cute, or exaggerated to the point of becoming caricatures.

DO NOT create:

- doodle / whiteboard stick figures
- sketchy marker drawings
- rough scribble characters
- ultra-flat icon-style illustrations
- realistic people
- photorealistic anatomy
- 3D characters
- Pixar-style 3D rendering
- anime / manga styling
- hyper-detailed comic-book realism
- glossy 3D digital artwork
- painterly realism
- overly cute chibi characters
- extremely exaggerated cartoon proportions
- dense fantasy artwork unless the narration requires it

The image should look like a professionally illustrated 2D cartoon frame, with strong visual clarity, personality and storytelling value.

=====================================================================
FACIAL DESIGN — SIMPLE, EXPRESSIVE CARTOON FACES
=====================================================================

Human faces should be clearly cartoonish and expressive rather than realistic.

Each face may include:

- large or medium expressive cartoon eyes with simple pupils
- simple eyebrows when useful for emotion
- a small simple cartoon nose
- a simple expressive mouth
- ears when visible in the character angle
- clean stylized facial shapes

Keep facial features simple, readable and consistent with the cartoon style.

Do NOT create:

- photorealistic facial anatomy
- realistic skin pores or facial texture
- hyper-detailed eyes
- complex wrinkles
- realistic lip rendering
- photographic facial shading
- uncanny realistic faces

Emotion should be communicated through a combination of:

- eye direction
- eyebrow position
- mouth shape
- head angle
- body posture
- arm position

Skin should use natural, clean cartoon skin tones appropriate to the character. Do NOT force every character to have a white face.

=====================================================================
HUMAN FIGURE DESIGN
=====================================================================

Every person must remain a simplified 2D cartoon figure.

HEAD:
- rounded or oval cartoon head
- clean bold outline
- simple expressive facial features
- no realistic anatomical rendering

HAIR:
- clean stylized hair masses
- clear silhouette
- simple internal strokes only where useful
- consistent hairstyle for recurring characters

BODY:
- simplified cartoon anatomy
- believable proportions
- slightly exaggerated for readability when appropriate
- natural standing, sitting, walking and action poses
- clearly defined clothing shapes

ARMS:
- simplified cartoon arms with clean outlines

LEGS:
- simplified cartoon legs with clean outlines

HANDS:
- simple cartoon hands with readable fingers when necessary
- avoid overly detailed anatomical hands

FEET:
- simple cartoon shoes or feet with clean silhouettes

The character should look like a finished 2D cartoon person, not a stick figure or doodle.

=====================================================================
CHARACTER DIFFERENTIATION
=====================================================================

If multiple people appear in the same image, they must be visually distinguishable.

Use:

- different hair shapes and hairstyles
- different skin tones when appropriate
- different clothing silhouettes
- different clothing colours
- different body height
- different body proportions
- different age cues
- distinctive glasses, hats, beards or accessories when relevant
- simple signature props

Facial features may vary naturally as part of the cartoon design, but do not rely on tiny facial details as the primary identity system.

Examples:

hunter → messy dark hair + rugged clothing + spear

gatherer → tied hair + practical clothing + basket

elder → grey hair + simple robe + walking stick

merchant → short cap + tunic + goods basket

worker → short hair + simple shirt + tool

Keep these characteristics consistent whenever that specific person returns.

=====================================================================
COLOUR STYLE
=====================================================================

Use a consistent colorful cartoon palette throughout the entire video.

Preferred visual palette:

- clear sky blue
- natural greens
- warm beige / cream
- earthy browns
- muted reds
- warm orange
- denim blue
- soft yellow
- natural skin tones
- dark charcoal / black outlines

Colours should feel:

- colorful
- clean
- balanced
- warm
- moderately saturated
- visually harmonious

The palette should have enough colour variety to make frames lively and engaging, while still feeling like the SAME video.

Avoid:

- neon colours
- fluorescent colours
- random rainbow palettes
- muddy or dirty colours
- excessive saturation
- excessive colour variety without a visual reason
- glossy candy-like rendering
- dull monochrome scenes

Use mostly flat fills with subtle controlled cartoon shading and small highlights.

Do NOT use heavy gradients, photorealistic lighting, or glossy 3D shading.

=====================================================================
BACKGROUND + ENVIRONMENT
=====================================================================

The environment should feel clearly illustrated and somewhat detailed, while remaining easy to read.

Use:

- recognizable architecture
- trees and vegetation
- roads and ground surfaces
- furniture
- vehicles
- tools
- household objects
- shops and buildings
- landscapes
- props relevant to the narration
- foreground and background elements that create depth

Backgrounds may contain more detail than a doodle illustration, but every element should support the scene.

Use clear environmental perspective and layered composition.

Add useful details such as:

- windows
- doors
- fences
- shelves
- tables
- plants
- signs without readable text
- road markings
- vehicle details
- simple environmental textures

Do NOT overcrowd the image.

Do NOT turn the background into photorealism.

Leave enough visual breathing room around the main character or main object.

The result should feel like a polished cartoon animation frame, not a children's activity sheet.

=====================================================================
LINE ART
=====================================================================

Use:

- bold clean black or near-black outlines
- smooth controlled cartoon linework
- slight organic variation in line weight
- crisp silhouettes
- clean internal detail
- clear separation between character, object and background
- subtle illustrated texture where useful

The linework should feel intentionally drawn and polished.

Do NOT use:

- rough marker scribbles
- sketchbook pencil lines
- scratchy construction lines
- watercolor outlines
- perfectly sterile vector-icon styling
- thin technical illustration lines
- photorealistic textures
- 3D rendering

The dark outline is a key part of the visual identity.

=====================================================================
CARTOON SHADING + DEPTH
=====================================================================

Use simple 2D cartoon shading to give characters and objects depth.

Preferred:

- subtle cel-style shadow areas
- gentle highlights
- soft ambient tonal variation
- simple contact shadows
- readable light direction
- clean flat colour regions with controlled shading

Avoid:

- realistic cinematic lighting
- complex photorealistic shadows
- glossy reflections everywhere
- metallic 3D rendering
- dramatic lens effects
- heavy bloom
- plastic-looking surfaces

The shading should support the illustration without overpowering the clean cartoon design.

 COMPOSITION  
\=====================================================================

Keep the composition similar to a documentary / educational animation.

Prefer:

\- medium-wide shots  
\- wide establishing shots  
\- medium shots  
\- occasional close-ups for important objects or emotions

Keep the primary subject clearly readable.

Avoid extreme zooms.

Avoid extremely distant shots where characters become tiny.

Avoid huge crowded panoramas unless the narration specifically describes  
a large settlement, city, battlefield, crowd or landscape.

Characters and important props should occupy a useful portion of the frame.

Do NOT use the exact same framing repeatedly for every beat.

However, do NOT create dramatic cinematic zooms simply for variety.

The video should still feel visually consistent.

\=====================================================================  
 DRAW THE BEAT  
\=====================================================================

Every image must clearly illustrate what the narration says.

If the narration describes:

a person → draw the person

an activity → draw the activity

an object → draw the object

a place → draw the place

a comparison → visually compare the two ideas

a historical process → show the process

an abstract concept → invent a simple visual metaphor

Never create a blank or generic frame.

Never add unrelated visual information merely to make the image look  
interesting.

The visual should communicate the narration immediately.

\=====================================================================  
 RECURRING CHARACTERS — FLOW \#NAME → @NAME WORKFLOW  
\=====================================================================

THIS IS A CRITICAL FLOW WORKFLOW RULE.

Flow uses:

\#Name

to CREATE and NAME a character reference image.

Flow uses:

@Name

to REFER BACK to that previously generated character in later scene prompts.

Therefore:

NEVER use @Name in a timeline scene unless the \#Name reference has already  
been generated.

A recurring-character reference is NOT a timeline frame.

It must be created FIRST.

\=====================================================================  
 HOW TO IDENTIFY RECURRING CHARACTERS  
\=====================================================================

Before writing ANY scene prompts, scan the ENTIRE timestamped script.

Identify every SPECIFIC PERSON who appears in TWO OR MORE visual beats.

Examples:

Richard Lee appearing multiple times  
Daniel appearing multiple times  
a specific grandmother appearing multiple times  
a specific hunter appearing multiple times

Only these recurring specific people receive \#Name references.

DO NOT create a character reference for every person.

DO NOT create references for:

\- generic crowds  
\- generic groups  
\- one-off people  
\- unnamed people appearing only once  
\- background figures

Examples of generic figures:

"three hunters"

"several women"

"a group of children"

"a crowd of villagers"

These should be described directly in the scene prompt.

\=====================================================================  
 IMPORTANT — DO NOT DESCRIBE RECURRING CHARACTERS AGAIN  
\=====================================================================

Once a recurring character reference has been generated:

DO NOT recreate that person's appearance using words in later prompts.

Do NOT write:

"the same man"

"the same anthropologist"

"Richard Lee with the same appearance"

"Richard Lee, short hair and beige shirt..."

Do NOT re-describe his face, hair, clothing or body.

Instead use:

The @Name reference controls the person's appearance.

For example:

@RichardLee observing the hunters...

@RichardLee holding his notebook...

This is the ONLY reliable way to maintain that specific person's identity  
across Flow images.

\=====================================================================  
 CHARACTER REFERENCE DEPENDENCY  
\=====================================================================

The workflow MUST happen in this order:

1\. Read the entire script.

2\. Identify recurring specific characters.

3\. Create \#Name reference prompts.

4\. Generate those \#Name images in Flow.

5\. Make sure those references exist and are available to Flow.

6\. Only then begin Stage 2\.

7\. Use @Name in every scene where that specific person appears.

NEVER skip step 3\.

NEVER use @Name before the reference exists.

NEVER rely on prose such as "same person" for recurring characters.

\=====================================================================

Example:

That person does not need @tag consistency because they only appear once.

Do NOT create role references for generic groups.

\=====================================================================  
 RED-CROSS RULE — NEGATION / ELIMINATION  
\=====================================================================

When the narration explicitly rules something OUT, use a bold red X.

Trigger words include:

no  
none  
not  
never  
without  
avoid  
eliminate  
ruled out  
wasn't  
didn't  
cannot  
couldn't

Draw the thing first.

Then add:

a BOLD RED X made from two thick hand-drawn red marker strokes.

The red X must clearly communicate the negation.

If the entire concept is negated:

cross the WHOLE IMAGE.

If only one element is negated:

cross ONLY that element.

State the exact scope in the prompt.

Red should be used specifically for the "no / eliminated / ruled out"  
visual signal.

Do not randomly add red X marks when the narration does not contain a  
meaningful negation.
